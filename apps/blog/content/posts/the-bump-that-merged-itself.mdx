At 02:20:00 on a Thursday, a bot opened a pull request bumping five packages. At 02:22:03 it
was on `main`. Two minutes and three seconds, no human awake, no review, five dependencies
into the branch every deploy comes from.

That should read as reckless. The interesting question is why it is not, and the answer is
not in that pull request at all. It is in the other ten.

## The record, in full

Dependabot has opened **sixteen** pull requests here. Three died to something unrelated: I
rewrote `main`'s history to purge a work-account identifier and their bases stopped existing.
That leaves **thirteen real encounters with the gate**.

Of those thirteen, the machine resolved three:

| PR | Packages | Created | Merged | Window |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [#314](https://github.com/climaa/acceptance-gate/pull/314) | 7 | 02:19:25 | 02:22:13 | 2m 48s |
| [#327](https://github.com/climaa/acceptance-gate/pull/327) | 4 | 18:04:59 | 18:07:34 | 2m 35s |
| [#390](https://github.com/climaa/acceptance-gate/pull/390) | 5 | 02:20:00 | 02:22:03 | 2m 03s |

Three out of thirteen. Before that number reads as a failure, note what the three have in
common: they are the same pull request. All three are the weekly `minor-and-patch` group,
arriving with a different payload each time. Every individual bump — which in this repo means
every major — went through a person.

**The automation did not make the decisions. It made ten of them cheap enough to be worth
making properly.**

## The third actor

I have written before about [the two paths to `main` and what protects them](/blog/the-guardrail-you-can-switch-off):
me, reaching for a shortcut on a bad afternoon, and an autonomous agent whose surface was
narrowed in advance so the shortcut was never available. The conclusion there was that rules
bind only until the person who wrote them deletes them, and the durable control is a
capability never granted.

Dependabot is neither of those actors, and the model has nothing to say about it.

It is not me, so narrowing my own habits does nothing. It is not the agent, whose token I
scoped myself and whose instructions I wrote. It is a third party pushing to a branch in my
repository, on a schedule I do not control, carrying a lockfile diff I did not author and
will not read line by line. Nobody is going to review 301 changed lines of `pnpm-lock.yaml`
at two in the morning, and pretending otherwise is how supply-chain assumptions get made by
default rather than on purpose.

So the question is not whether I trust the bot. It is what a `yes` has to clear before it
means anything.

## What the yes waits for

`gh pr merge --auto --squash`. The flag is the whole mechanism: it does not merge, it parks
the request until the required check reports green, and the required check is a single
aggregator named `gate` that fails unless every job it depends on succeeded. That
indirection, and why a skipped check has to count as red, is [documented next to the diagram
of the two paths](https://acceptance-gate-storybook.vercel.app/index.html?path=/docs/docs-devops-the-gate--docs).

For #390 the legs were lint, typecheck, build, test, format, a cognitive-complexity ceiling,
the sandcastle suite and the full end-to-end run. All green, `gate` closed at 02:21:58, and
the merge fired five seconds later.

One honest caveat, because it matters more than the list does: passing that is evidence about
compilation, types, behaviour and complexity. It is not evidence that the bump is a good idea.
Those are different claims, and the rest of this post is about the gap between them.

## The ten that did not

Here is the part that never gets published, because publishing refusals requires having
measured them.

[#221](https://github.com/climaa/acceptance-gate/pull/221) offered ESLint 10. It fails because
`eslint-plugin-react` and `eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y` both cap their peer ranges at ESLint 9 and
still call `context.getFilename()`, an API ESLint 10 removed.
[#223](https://github.com/climaa/acceptance-gate/pull/223) offered TypeScript 7 and died on
`TS5102: Option 'baseUrl' has been removed`, with `typescript-eslint` refusing TS 7 outright
for good measure. Both are blocked upstream. No diff makes either work.

Closing them would have been the obvious move, and the wrong one — Dependabot would reopen
both the following week, forever. Each instead became an entry in
[`dependabot.yml`](https://github.com/climaa/acceptance-gate/blob/main/.github/dependabot.yml)
carrying the issue number, the date it was last re-verified against a real failing run, and
the condition that takes it back out. The commands that decide when an entry expires live in
the issue, not the config, so removing the entry without running them is a step somebody has
to take deliberately.

That file now distinguishes three kinds of refusal, and the distinction is the useful part:

| Kind | Means | Comes out when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blanket | The bump is a hand-authored issue, never a bot PR | Someone decides to do the work |
| Majors, blocked upstream | No diff makes it work | The issue's re-check commands pass |
| Majors, pinned to this repo | Not wrong — early. It tracks a value here | The same commit that moves that value |

The third category is the one I did not anticipate needing, and it is where the interesting
failure lives.

## The one no layer could have caught

[#315](https://github.com/climaa/acceptance-gate/pull/315) offered `@types/node` 22 to 26.
Every check that matters went green. `typecheck` in particular: five packages, clean.

That green is the finding, not the reassurance.

`engines` pins Node to `>=22.14.0 <23.0.0`, `.nvmrc` says `22.14.0`, and every CI job installs
from `.nvmrc`. Types describe a runtime. On `@types/node` 26, `tsc` accepts Node 26 APIs that
throw on the Node 22 that actually runs the code. **The typecheck leg passed precisely because
it had been pointed at a different runtime than the one under test.**

I have asked a version of this question before. [The migration that passed CI](/blog/the-migration-that-passed-ci)
ends on it: if every colour in the app shifted one scheme sideways tomorrow, which test would
fail? That post has an answer — none of the compiled layers, and the visual one. The rubric is
*did not catch it* against *caught it*, and the lesson is that a layer was missing.

Ask the same question of this bump and the answer is different in kind. Not "no test failed,
so build a better layer". There is no layer to build. A type-level assertion cannot detect that
it has been handed the wrong runtime description, because the description is the thing it
reasons from. You cannot write a test that catches its own premise being swapped.

So the fix was not a test. It was a policy entry, scoped to majors so minor and patch keep
flowing, and `22.20.1` is where the 22 line ends anyway — DefinitelyTyped published it last on
2026-08-07 — which makes it a stable pin rather than rot. It comes out in the same commit that
moves `.nvmrc` off 22. Never before it, and never on its own.

A gate that only ever produces green or red cannot express that. A written refusal can.

## When the gate is the thing that is wrong

Three times the failing component was not the bump. It was the automation.

**The secret in the wrong store.** On
[#220](https://github.com/climaa/acceptance-gate/pull/220) every leg passed except `auto-merge`.
The merge runs on a personal token rather than `GITHUB_TOKEN`, because a `GITHUB_TOKEN` merge
does not trigger further workflows and the push-to-`main` run that seeds the shared build cache
was being skipped on every bot merge. But that token has to live in the **Dependabot** secret
store, not Actions. Put it in the wrong one and it looks correct in the UI, arrives as an empty
string, and `gh` fails with an auth error that never mentions the word "store".

The fix was not to move the secret quietly. It was a `preflight` job whose only purpose is to
fail with the answer in the log:

```text
::error::GH_TOKEN is empty, so auto-merge cannot authenticate.
::error::Add it under Settings > Secrets and variables > Dependabot.
::error::A secret of the same name under Actions does NOT reach Dependabot-triggered runs.
```

**The guard aimed at the wrong sample.** That `preflight` job gates on the pull request's
author, never on the actor who triggered the run. Gating it on the actor as well seemed
tidier and made it silent at the one moment it was needed: pressing "Update branch" on a bot
PR flips the actor to a human, the job skips, and the checks list reads "all passed, 1
skipped" instead of naming the missing secret. That is not hypothetical — #218, #222 and #225
each show `auto-merge` skipped, and each was merged by hand minutes after somebody pressed
that button.

**The check that was green having done nothing.** On #219 and #224 the `auto-merge` check
reports pass. Neither was merged by it. Both are majors, the merge step is conditional on the
update type, and the job log ends without ever opening the `Run gh pr merge` group. The job
correctly declined — and reported the same colour it reports when it succeeds. From the checks
list you cannot tell "merged it" from "looked at it and refused".

All three are one defect. A check can be green for reasons that have nothing to do with what a
reader assumes it is asserting. The clearest instance in this repo is not in CI at all — it is
an accessibility scenario that scanned "the first article" on the blog index:

> the newest published post happened to be the only one carrying no code fences, so the
> post-page scenario stayed green for a year of publishing while never once putting a
> highlighted token in front of axe.

Same shape, different domain, a year apart. That is what makes it a rule rather than an
anecdote: **a check pointed at the wrong sample passes for reasons unrelated to its claim,
and it passes quietly.**

## What the two minutes actually buy

Not speed. Nobody needed those dependencies at 02:22 rather than over coffee, and the whole
exercise saves an afternoon a year at best.

What it buys is that a dependency bump is **one class of decision instead of thirteen
conversations**. Did the gate pass? If yes, it lands and I read about it later. If no, the
refusal gets written down somewhere the next bump will meet it, and I never have that
conversation again.

That second half is the load-bearing one, and it is the half that gets left out of posts about
auto-merge. A gate that only knows how to say yes is a rubber stamp with extra steps. Thirteen
encounters produced ten refusals here, and not one of them has had to be re-litigated — not
because the tooling is clever, but because each `no` cost the twenty minutes it takes to write
down what failed, what would have to change, and who decides.

The confidence to leave it running unattended does not come from the three that merged
themselves. It comes from knowing exactly what the other ten cost, because the answer is
written in a file I can read.
